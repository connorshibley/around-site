Research/Unit 3

Marketing Research Challenge

Marketing Decision:

We face the question of whether Ar0und should put its first marketing dollars into the on-premise bartender partnership program (training, incentives, in-venue placement with partner bars) before spending comparable money on retail distribution and digital or social advertising.

Research Problem:

Most of our go-to-market plan rests on the claim that the experience of a bartender personally recommending the drink, does more to convert a hesitant customer than anything else, more than menu placement, price, or an ad would. We know the category is growing and we know our word of mouth matters to our customers, but we don’t know how much a bartender’s in-person recommendation actually moves someone from consideration to ordering, compared to just reading a good menu description or seeing a lower price.

Research Objective:

To find out how much a bartender’s personal, in-person recommendation actually changes a target customer’s odds of ordering an unfamiliar non-alcoholic cocktail, relative to seeing the same drink listed on a menu or a store shelf with no recommendation.

Hypothesis:

Adults 21-34 who drink once a week or less are more likely to order a new non-alcoholic cocktail when a bartender recommends it than when they only see it on a menu or a store shelf.

Secondary Research:

The market exists and is growing fast. Non-alcoholic beer, wine and spirits passed $1 billion in U.S. store sales in 2025 (NIQ, via BeverageDaily). Ready-to-drink NA cocktails grew 123% in the year to August 2025 and now make up 64% of NA spirits sales (NIQ). Independent restaurants added NA drinks to their menus 47% more in January 2026 than a year earlier (SpotOn, via Business Wire).

Our target still goes out, and they listen to bartenders. Adults 21 to 34 went out for a drink 5.7 times in three months, versus 1.4 times for adults 55 and older. 38% of Gen Z ask for the bartender's recommendation every or almost every time they buy a cocktail, 10 points above the average guest, and 74% of bartenders say guests order what they recommend (CGA by NIQ). Legal-age Gen Z isn't quitting either: 74% had a drink in the past six months and 84% had a mixed drink (YPulse, citing IWSR, 2026). IWSR counts only legal-age drinkers, which is why it runs higher than Gallup's 50% for all 18 to 34 year olds on Page 1. At bars, NA cocktails are already the most-ordered NA drink: 9% of guests had one in the past three months (NIQ, 2025).

State laws differ. Under federal rules, drinks under 0.5% ABV are not alcoholic beverages and can be labeled "non-alcoholic" or "alcohol free," so many NA drinks contain trace alcohol. Ar0und is a true 0.0%. There is no national rule on who can buy NA drinks: 39 states had no statewide policy on selling them to minors (NPR, 2024). Michigan sells NA beer only to people 18 and older but requires no license for NA wine and spirits under 0.5% (Michigan Liquor Control Commission). In New York, bill S3836 would let licensed liquor stores sell NA versions of alcoholic drinks. For Ar0und, that means checking the rules in each launch state, selling 21+ only, and leaning on bars and grocery stores before liquor stores in New York.

The gap: none of this tells us whether a bartender's word moves our target to order a new NA cocktail instead of just reading the menu or seeing it on a shelf. That is the question our survey tests.

Primary Research Need:

Before we commit real money to bartender training and royalties instead of a cheaper paid social push or a retail-first launch, we need primary data from our own target. We started with a short online survey (Page 3) because it is the fastest and cheapest of the three primary methods. If the survey supports the hypothesis, the next steps are an ethnographic study and a simple A/B test in two or three partner bars, comparing how often guests order Ar0und when a bartender recommends it versus when it only sits on the menu.

What has to be true for our plan to work:

Our hypothesis is the main test, but three other things also have to hold before the bartender plan is worth the money. The survey checks each one:

1. The hypothesis itself: a bartender recommendation beats a menu or a store shelf

2. Reach: our target goes out often enough to meet a bartender .

3. Repeat: after a first try at the bar, they would buy again somewhere we sell

4. Positioning: global recipes and a not-too-sweet taste are what they care about

The hypothesis can be true and still not justify the budget if reach or repeat fails. So these extra checks build on it: they decide how much to put behind bartenders, not whether bartenders matter. If the hypothesis fails, that budget moves to retail distribution and paid content before launch, not after.
